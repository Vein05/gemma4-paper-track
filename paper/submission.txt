## Abstract

Coding agents often decide whether a patch is finished by asking a model, often the one that wrote it. Studies of self-preference test whether a judge is biased toward its own outputs by comparing its scores on them with its scores on other models' outputs. The closest study controls for how hard each problem is, but not for how strong the other writers are. We ask Gemma 4 31B and eight other models to score SWE-bench patches, and we grade every patch with the hidden tests. By the usual measure, Gemma trusts its own failing patches far more than other writers' failing patches. Against writers as strong as Gemma, the gap shrinks on our main data set and is not detectable on two others. Gemma gives the top score to 61% of Kimi-K2's failing patches, almost the same share as its own. Most of Gemma's own failing patches are wrong fixes that pass every existing test. Existing tests catch few of these wrong fixes, and when Gemma is shown an existing test that asserts the behavior the issue changes, it obeys the test and breaks the correct patch. We release every agent and judge trace on [GitHub](https://github.com/Vein05/gemma4-paper-track) and [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/vein05/gemma4-paper-track-data).

## 1. Introduction

In matplotlib issue 24570, Gemma 4 31B wrote a patch that fixes the reported alignment bug but also swaps the left and right cases of a shared helper. When we later asked Gemma whether this patch would pass the project's hidden tests, it gave the highest score on our 0 to 100 scale and called the patch "a simple swap" that "correctly implements the expected behavior". GLM-4.5 scored the same patch 20 because it "breaks VPacker by reversing left and right", and the hidden tests for left and right alignment fail. Coding agents make this call at the end of every task, and many make it by asking the model that wrote the patch.

The standard test of this risk compares a judge's scores on its own outputs with its scores on other models' outputs. Pombal et al. (2026) ran this test on contest programs with executed labels. Most judges favored their own programs, the preference held within each band of problem difficulty, and it was larger when the judge recognized its own output. Gemma 3 12B and 27B were the exception. The test has a second confound that difficulty bands do not remove. Errors from strong writers are harder for a verifier to detect (Zhou et al., 2025), so a judge compared with weaker writers will seem to favor itself even if it never recognizes its own work.

We ran this test on Gemma 4 31B with SWE-bench patches from five other agents. By the usual measure, Gemma scores its own failing patches 16.3 points further above judges from other model families than it scores other writers' failing patches. Gemma gives Kimi-K2's failing patches the top score about as often as its own, and Llama-4-Maverick's a quarter as often. Against writers at least as strong as Gemma, the gap shrinks on our held-out issues and is within noise on SWE-bench Lite and on Go, Ruby and PHP issues.

Part of what looks like self-preference is thus trust in strong writers' failing patches. Most of Gemma's own failing patches are wrong fixes: the issue's tests fail while every other existing test passes. Neither the checks that Gemma runs itself nor the existing tests catch most of them. The existing tests do catch regressions, and Gemma repairs many of these when shown the broken test. When a test asserts the old behavior that the issue asks to change, however, Gemma obeys the test and undoes a correct fix.

We make the following contributions:

- **Measured self-preference depends on who the other writers are.** Compared only with writers as strong as itself, Gemma's extra trust in its own failing patches falls from 16.3 to 9.2 points on our held-out issues, and it is within noise on two other data sets (Section 3.1).
- **Gemma gives the top score to most strong writers' failing patches in Python, Go, Ruby and PHP.** Most of its own are wrong fixes, and the checks we ran through Gemma missed most of them (Section 3.2).
- **Existing tests catch regressions, but outdated tests mislead the repair.** Shown the existing tests its patch breaks, Gemma repairs real regressions. On Lite it breaks 12 of 14 correct patches held back by a test that asserts the old behavior. On other writers' patches, having Gemma label outdated tests first spares most correct patches the repair would break (Section 3.3).

## 2. Setup

**Patches.** The main data set is 193 held-out issues, a random sample of SWE-bench Verified issues (Jimenez et al., 2023) outside SWE-bench Verified Mini (MariusHobbhahn). We call the model that wrote a patch its writer. The 4-bit competition checkpoint of Gemma 4 31B made one attempt per issue in mini-SWE-agent v1.7.0 (SWE-agent) at temperature 0, and we graded its patches with SWE-bench's grading code. Other writers' patches are public mini-SWE-agent submissions (SWE-bench experiments) by GLM-4.5, gpt-oss-120b, Kimi-K2, Llama-4-Maverick and Qwen3-Coder, with their official labels. We removed test files from every patch and dropped very long patches, leaving 180 from Gemma.

Two more data sets test whether the result depends on our run or our language. Another team ran Gemma 4 31B on the 300 SWE-bench Lite issues, in mini-SWE-agent 2.2.1 at full precision with thinking on. We ran Gemma on 120 Go, Ruby and PHP issues from SWE-bench Multilingual (SWE-bench) in mini-SWE-agent 2.0.0 with thinking on. There the other writers are four recent models, GLM-5, Kimi-K2.5, DeepSeek-V3.2 and MiniMax-M2.5.

**Judges.** Nine models scored every held-out patch: Gemma 4 31B, gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.5-9B, GLM-4.7-Flash and the five writers above. Each judge saw the issue and one patch, without its writer, and scored the chance that the patch passes the hidden tests from 0 to 100. Calls went through OpenRouter at temperature 0 with reasoning off (gpt-oss at low effort). Fewer judges scored the Lite and Go, Ruby and PHP patches, chosen before any score was read.

**Measures.** A judge's score on a failing patch says little alone, because some failing patches look right to everyone. We therefore measure how far a judge scores each patch above the mean of the outside judges, the judges from other model families. Extra trust is how much further above them a judge scores one group of failing patches than another group. A strong writer is one that solves at least as many issues as Gemma on the same data set. We report 95% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of issues.

SWE-bench grades a patch with two groups of tests. The issue's tests fail before the reference fix and pass after it. The other existing tests must keep passing. We call a failing patch a wrong fix when the issue's tests fail and every other test passes, and a regression when it breaks one of the other tests.

**Plans.** We committed a written plan, with the rule for reading its result, before computing each test. The split by writer strength and the split by failure type were chosen after we saw results, and we mark them as exploratory.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Measured self-preference depends on who the other writers are

Against all other writers, Gemma's extra trust in its own failing patches is 16.3 points on the held-out issues and 17.8 points on Lite (Figure 1b). Table 1 shows where the held-out gap comes from. Gemma gives 100 to 60% of its own failing held-out patches and to 61% of Kimi-K2's. For the two weakest writers the share falls to a quarter or less, and these two writers supply 59% of the other writers' failing patches.

**Table 1.** Gemma 4 31B's scores on failing held-out patches, by writer (exploratory). "Above outside judges" is Gemma's mean score minus the outside judges' mean on the same patches, in points.

| Writer | Patches passing | Failing patches | Gemma scores 100 | Above outside judges |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qwen3-Coder | 70% | 46 | 48% | +1.4 |
| GLM-4.5 | 62% | 61 | 56% | +8.9 |
| Kimi-K2 | 61% | 54 | 61% | +7.1 |
| Gemma 4 31B | 54% | 82 | 60% | +13.7 |
| gpt-oss-120b | 30% | 109 | 25% | -6.7 |
| Llama-4-Maverick | 27% | 123 | 16% | -10.7 |

Against strong writers alone, the gap shrinks on both data sets where we can split them (Figure 1b). On the held-out issues it falls to 9.2 points, and the drop's 95% interval excludes zero. On Lite, Claude 4 Sonnet is the only other writer as strong as Gemma, and Gemma trusts Claude's failing patches about as much as its own (+1.7 points). On Go, Ruby and PHP every other writer is strong, and the gap is 4.2 points with an interval that includes zero. Our plan for that run had predicted a gap above zero.

![Gemma's trust in each writer's failing patches, and its extra trust in its own against all and strong writers](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Vein05/gemma4-paper-track/main/paper/figures/fig-writers.png)

**Figure 1.** Gemma 4 31B trusts failing patches more when their writer is stronger. (a) Held-out issues. Shaded writers solve at least as many issues as Gemma, and dot area shows the number of failing patches. (b) Gemma's extra trust in its own failing patches, with 95% intervals, shrinks against strong writers.

A smaller gap remains on the held-out issues, and two results point to the patch content as its source. On Lite, Gemma gives other writers' failing patches 20 points more extra trust when they make nearly the same edit as Gemma's. The same planned test is inconclusive on the held-out issues. If the trust came from the model family, Gemma 4 12B and 26B-A4B would share it, but they show no measurable extra trust in the 31B's failing patches (Supplement, Section D). We never varied the writer with the patch held fixed, so we cannot rule out that Gemma recognizes its own patches.

### 3.2 Gemma trusts strong writers' failing patches, and its own are mostly wrong fixes

Gemma trusts strong writers' failing patches in Go, Ruby and PHP too. On Go, Ruby and PHP, Gemma gives 100 to 76% of its own failing patches and to 68% of the four other writers' failing patches. The outside judges give Gemma's failing patches 100 only 29% of the time. There, Gemma's score separates its own passing patches from its failing ones no better than chance.

What are these patches? Most of Gemma's own are wrong fixes. We sorted Gemma's 82 failing held-out patches by failure type (exploratory; Supplement, Section C). 52 are wrong fixes, in which every existing test still passes. 13 pass the issue's tests but break another, and 17 fail both. Gemma's extra trust is about the same for wrong fixes and for regressions, and the outside judges also score both kinds alike.

Checks that Gemma runs itself miss most of the same failures (Table 2; Supplement, Section E). Gemma's thinking mode did not help, although Chen et al. (2025) found that long chains of thought before evaluation reduce harmful self-preference.

**Table 2.** Checks run by Gemma, and what happens to its failing patches.

| Check run by Gemma | What happens to Gemma's failing patches |
|---|---|
| Thinking before scoring (Verified Mini issues) | still scores 62% of its failing patches 100, against 61% without thinking |
| Its own reproduction script, written while fixing the issue | the last run ends without an error in almost all failing runs that ran one |
| A shell with the patch applied; Gemma must write a test that fails without it | on the 49 failing held-out patches it had scored 100, still scores 79% at 100 |
| Fine-tuning Gemma (LoRA) on labeled patches from other issues | still accepts almost all failing patches it had scored 100, on new held-out and Lite issues |

### 3.3 Existing tests catch regressions, but outdated tests mislead the repair

A wrong fix passes every test on SWE-bench's list, so a check built on existing tests mostly reaches regressions. Our check runs the existing tests, on that list or not, that match the changed code before and after the patch, and holds back the patch if a test that passed before now fails. A held-back patch gets one more Gemma agent run. In one condition Gemma sees the broken tests and is asked to make them pass and keep the issue solved without editing the test files. In the other, the second look, Gemma is only told that the patch may have mistakes (Supplement, Section F).

The check holds back 22 of Gemma's 30 failing held-out patches that break a listed test, and only 4 of its 52 wrong fixes. Shown the broken tests, Gemma repairs 14 of the 26 held-back failing patches, against 7 with a second look (Table 3). On Gemma's Lite patches, the same repair fixed 1 of 30. Half of those 30 were wrong fixes. Their broken tests are outside SWE-bench's list, so making them pass cannot fix the issue.

**Table 3.** The repair on two sets of Gemma's patches, with the broken tests shown and with a second look. A held-back patch "breaks an existing test" when it fails one of SWE-bench's listed regression tests. Repaired counts leave out one Lite issue whose test fails at random.

| | Held-out, tests | Held-out, second look | Lite, tests | Lite, second look |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Held-back failing patches | 26 | 26 | 30 | 30 |
| Of these, break an existing test | 22 | | 15 | |
| Repaired | 14 | 7 | 1 | 0 |
| Held-back correct patches | 9 | 9 | 14 | 14 |
| Broken by the repair | 4 | 1 | 12 | 2 |
| Net change in passing patches | +10 | +6 | −11 | −2 |

The larger cost falls on correct patches. For all 14 correct Lite patches that the check held back, the reference fix fails the same check, so the test asserts the old behavior. Gemma broke 12 of these 14 patches when shown such a test, against 2 with a second look. On xarray issue 5131 it wrote that "the constraints of the task (making tests pass without editing them) force me to restore it". On held-out issues the repair still helps on net: 108 of Gemma's 180 patches pass after it, against 98 before. On Lite it hurts.

The missing step is a decision about which tests may change, which Agentless (Xia et al., 2024) leaves to the model. We tested one rule under a written plan, on 172 held-back patches from the other five writers. Gemma first labels each broken test as outdated or a real regression from the issue and the test report alone. The repair then ignores outdated tests, or is skipped if all are. Correct patches broken fell from 20 to 7, but 5 fewer failing patches were repaired. The net gain of 8 passing patches is within noise, which our plan counts as failure.

## 4. Related work

**Measuring self-preference.** Panickssery et al. (2024) link a judge's preference for its own summaries to how well it recognizes them. Pombal et al. (2026) asked judges whether a LiveCodeBench program passes each unit test. Most judges wrongly pass their own programs more often than other families' programs. Roytburg et al. (2026) compare a judge's own wrong answers with other models' wrong answers to the same question, and much of the measured self-preference goes away. Their correction concerns the judge. Ours concerns the other writers, and it applies even when the judge and the problems stay the same. Kozman et al. (2026) report both effects: a model that wrote an answer "approves its own errors 11.9 percentage points more often", and stronger generators' errors are harder to catch. Our results suggest the second can inflate measures of the first.

**Verifying strong writers.** On math, knowledge and reasoning tasks, Zhou et al. (2025) find that weak generators' errors are easier to detect than strong ones'. Table 1 shows the same pattern for repository patches. Khullar et al. (2026) find that one model rates a SWE-bench patch as more correct when it is framed as its own earlier answer, a cue our judges never see.

**Tests as a check.** In test-based program repair, test suites are often too weak, so repair tools produce many incorrect patches that pass them (Xiong et al., 2017). Our wrong fixes are this class. When code changes, some tests must change too, and Shang et al. (2026) find that coding agents identify those tests with an F1 below 50%. ImpossibleBench (Zhong et al., 2025) edits SWE-bench tests to conflict with the issue and tells the agent to put the issue first. Our conflicts come from the repositories' own tests, and our note asked for both without ranking them.

## 5. Limitations

The writer-strength and failure-type splits are exploratory, and the strong group holds one to four writers per data set. The own-patch gap against strong writers stays above zero on the held-out issues. The Go, Ruby and PHP run also changes the agent version, thinking and the language, so it supports the writer account without isolating it. In Gemma's held-out agent runs, the repository's Python environment was not active, so Gemma often could not import the code. This lowers its resolve rate and may produce more crash patches, which the repair fixes most easily. The repair's harm rests on 23 correct patches, and each Lite repair condition ran once.

## 6. Conclusion

A judge that seems to favor its own patches may partly be favoring strong writers. For Gemma 4 31B, the measured preference for its own failing patches shrinks, or is no longer detectable, once the comparison writers are as strong as Gemma, so self-preference studies should report who the other writers were. For an agent, the risk is Gemma's trust in wrong fixes, which neither its own checks nor the existing tests catch. An agent built on Gemma should not let Gemma's score decide that a patch is finished. When it adds the existing tests, it also needs a rule for which tests the issue may change.
