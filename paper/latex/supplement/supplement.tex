Its sections follow the paper. Sections~\ref{app:prompt} and~\ref{app:grading} give the setup of the paper's

\section{Setup details and judge prompt}
\label{app:prompt}

\paragraph{Issues.} The held-out issues are the first 200 of a fixed random order (seed 0) of SWE-bench Verified issues
outside the Mini issues for which at least one of the five other writers has a usable patch (447 issues). Five pilot
issues drawn first were left out of the sample. A usable patch is non-empty after removing test files and at most
30,000 characters long.

\paragraph{Agent.} The 4-bit competition checkpoint \texttt{gemma-4-31B-it-qat-w4a16-ct} (revision \texttt{52f3f65b}) ran
in mini-SWE-agent v1.7.0 with its SWE-bench configuration unchanged (250 steps, temperature 0, 60-second command
timeout), served by vLLM with a 131,072-token context on one NVIDIA L40S. Docker was replaced by Apptainer sandboxes
of the same SWE-bench images. Grading used SWE-bench 4.1.0's grading code in the same kind of sandbox.

\paragraph{Judges.} Outside judges for a model exclude its own family and, for each patch, the family of the patch's % prose-ok: number-dense-paragraph
writer. Gemma as judge is the hosted model. OpenRouter routed its 1,064 held-out calls to nine providers. Most of them
(983) went to providers that listed 4-bit or 8-bit weights on 2026-10-05. Of the rest, 79 went to providers that listed
no precision and 2 to a provider that served bf16. We did not record the precision of each call (provider per call: \path{scripts/derived/fresh_gemma4-31b.jsonl}).
In an earlier one-word check on 173 other patches, the hosted model pinned to a bf16 provider and the 4-bit checkpoint
gave the same answer in 331 of 344 calls. The re-ask limit was 8,000 output tokens for our own
patch sets and 3,000 for the mixed-harness set. Other writers' patches on the Mini issues keep their mixed-harness
scores.

\paragraph{Gemma as judge, served locally.} We repeated the held-out judging with the competition checkpoint itself,
served by vLLM 0.30.0 on one NVIDIA A100 at temperature 0 with thinking off, under a plan fixed before any score
(\path{spec/gemma-31b-local-judge.md}). It gave a score to all 1,063 prompts on the first ask (rows:
\path{scripts/derived/fresh_gemma4-31b-local.jsonl}).

On the paper's patches, the local judge gives 100 to 54\% of Gemma's failing patches and to 32\% of other writers'. % prose-ok: number-dense-paragraph
The hosted model gives 61\% and 35\%. On failing patches, the local judge's leniency difference is $+13.4$
[6.9, 20.0]. For the hosted model it is $+16.6$ [10.3, 23.1]. The paired difference, hosted minus local, is $+3.2$
[$-0.5$, 7.2]. We detect no difference between the two judges, but the interval allows the hosted model to be up to
7.2 points more lenient. On passing patches, the local judge's leniency difference is $+5.0$ [0.8, 9.0]. For the
hosted model it is $+3.4$ [$-2.0$, 8.0]. The local judge's AUROC is 0.658 on Gemma's patches and 0.762 on other
writers'. For the hosted model they are 0.622 and 0.751.

\paragraph{Prompt.} Every judge received the same system and user messages at temperature 0
(\path{scripts/selfjudge_matrix.py}, \path{scripts/selection_test.py}). Braces mark the parts filled in per patch.
\begin{promptbox}\ttfamily
\textbf{System:} You are an expert Python maintainer. You review a proposed fix for a reported issue. You cannot run
code; judge from the material given.\par\medskip
\textbf{User:} A user reported the issue below for the repository \{owner/repo\}.\par
<issue>\{issue text\}</issue>\par
A coding agent produced the patch below.\par
<patch>\{patch without test files\}</patch>\par
Question: after this patch is applied, will the maintainers' hidden tests for this issue pass? The hidden tests were
written for the upstream fix. They check the behaviour the issue asks for, and tests that passed before must still
pass. Explain briefly, then end your reply with a final line that is exactly SCORE: <n>, where n is your probability
from 0 to 100 that the hidden tests pass.
\end{promptbox}

\paragraph{Two measures used only in this supplement.} A judge's AUROC on a set of patches is the chance that it
scores a random passing patch above a random failing one, with ties counted as half. The self-judging gap is how much
a model's AUROC drops from other writers' patches to its own, minus the mean drop of the outside judges on the same
patches. A positive gap means the model separates its own passing and failing patches worse than the outside judges
do.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the re-ask (exploratory).} Leaving out patches whose answer needed a re-ask, the held-out
self-judging gap is $+0.030$ [$-0.024$, 0.083] (all judges' re-asked patches; 763 patches, 143 of Gemma's), $+0.055$
[0.001, 0.106] (only Kimi-K2's) and $+0.063$ [0.012, 0.115] (only Qwen3.5-9B's). Without the re-asked patches,
Gemma's own AUROC rises from 0.622 to 0.670, its leniency on failing patches is $+11.1$ [3.8, 18.2] and its
rubber-stamp rate 56\%. On per-judge percentile ranks instead of raw scores, the held-out leniency is $+0.085$
[0.048, 0.122] on failing patches and $+0.024$ [$-0.007$, 0.055] on passing patches.

\section{Agent runs and grading}
\label{app:grading}

\paragraph{Agent runs.} The 200 held-out issues ran as two jobs of 100. In the first, 98 runs submitted a patch, one
ran out of the 131,072-token context and one stopped after repeated model-call timeouts. Three patches were empty. In the second, one submitted patch was empty. On the Mini issues, 49 runs
submitted and one stopped after repeated timeouts. Empty patches and runs that stopped count as unresolved, as in
SWE-bench. One Mini issue (\texttt{django\_\_django-11848}) failed to start its sandbox and was run again. The Mini
analyses use the second run's patch.

\paragraph{Reference-patch check.} We graded the official reference patch of every issue we ran (5 pilot, 200
held-out, 50 Mini). 248 of 255 resolve in our environment. The first pass labelled 16 matplotlib and xarray issues as
timeouts. Logs showed that the container build, not the tests, exceeded a 1,200-second limit, and with a 3,600-second build
limit all but three resolved. The 7 issues whose reference patch does not resolve were dropped from the held-out set
(\texttt{astropy-7606}, \texttt{matplotlib-20826}, \texttt{-23314}, \texttt{-26113}, \texttt{-26208},
\texttt{requests-2931}, \texttt{sphinx-9711}). Gemma resolved none of them.

\paragraph{Agreement with official labels.} Before using our grading, we graded 30 gpt-oss-120b patches with known
official labels, stratified by repository. 26 of 30 matched. The four misses were environment failures (a
network-dependent test, Ghostscript file access) and patches that apply only with fuzzy matching on today's rebuilt
images. Other writers' patches therefore keep their official labels.

\paragraph{Resolve rates.} Gemma resolved 56 and 42 of the two held-out batches of 100, 98 of the 193 kept issues in
all, and 22 of the 50 Mini issues. On the Mini issues two sphinx patches ran past the 1,800-second test limit.
Counting them as failing, as SWE-bench does, changes the matched-harness gap from $+0.066$ to $+0.081$ [$-0.010$,
0.178].

\paragraph{Leftover files.} Gemma's held-out patches often add a file it left behind, such as a reproduction script
or a test project, and every judge sees that file in the diff. We removed every added file from all 957 held-out
patches and had all nine judges score them again with the same prompt and settings, under a plan fixed before the
scores (\path{spec/stripped-files-rescore.md}, \path{derived/stripped_files_rescore_2026-11-03.json}). On the 875
patches every judge scored, Gemma's leniency difference on failing patches is $+16.1$ [8.9, 23.2] without the added
files and $+18.3$ [10.8, 25.1] with them. The paired difference is $+2.2$ [$-4.6$, 8.7]. On unchanged patches, a
judge's score moves by 6 to 16 points between two runs, so this shift is within run-to-run noise.

\subsection*{Two more data sets}

We ran the paper's measures on two data sets outside SWE-bench Verified. Each had a written plan before any judge call
(\texttt{spec/third-party-lite.md}, \texttt{spec/livecodebench-judging.md}). The rule in both plans was the same. The
result holds if Gemma gives 100 to a larger share of its own failing items than of other writers' failing items, and
the leniency difference on failing items has a 95\% interval above zero. Both results hold (Table~\ref{tab:more}).

\paragraph{SWE-bench Lite, another team's run.} Gemma 4 31B's patches for the 300 SWE-bench Lite issues come from a
run that another team published on Hugging Face (\path{dougalldeepmind/2026-10-10-swebench-gemma4-lite-gemma-v2}, % prose-ok: banned
revision \texttt{1c5f3d3b}). We had no part in that run. It used the full-precision model with thinking on, sampled at
temperature 1.0 (top-p 0.95), and ran mini-SWE-agent 2.2.1 with one attempt per issue. That team graded the patches,
and 166 of 300 resolved their issue. We used their labels. The other writers are four entries of the SWE-bench Lite
leaderboard (\texttt{SWE-bench/experiments}): DeepSeek-V3 in Moatless, SWE-Fixer with Qwen2.5-72B, entroPO with
Qwen3-Coder-30B, and Claude 4 Sonnet in SWE-agent. We prepared all patches as in the paper. Of the 300 issues, 37 are
also held-out issues and 7 are Mini issues, with different patches. We kept them.

\paragraph{LiveCodeBench, no agent.} We generated Gemma's programs ourselves with vLLM, from the 4-bit competition
checkpoint, at temperature 0 with thinking off, using LiveCodeBench's own prompt (release\_v6, 1,055 problems;
\texttt{spec/livecodebench-pilot.md}). Answers cut off at the default 2,000-token limit got a second try at 8,000
tokens. Gemma then passes 873 of the 1,055 problems (82.7\%). We judged all 146 problems where Gemma's program ran and
failed, and 146 problems where it passed, drawn within difficulty. The other writers are DeepSeek-V3 and Claude 3.5
Sonnet, with the programs they released on LiveCodeBench. We removed comments and docstrings from every program,
because Gemma's long docstrings would tell a judge who wrote it. The prompt is the paper's, with a contest problem in
place of the issue, a program in place of the patch, and a competitive programmer in place of the Python maintainer in
the system prompt.

\paragraph{Judges.} Our remaining OpenRouter credit did not cover nine judges. Before any score was read, we left out
GLM-4.5 and Kimi-K2 on SWE-bench Lite, which leaves seven judges. On LiveCodeBench we also left out Qwen3-Coder as a
judge and GPT-4o as a writer, which leaves six judges. Of 1,478 Lite patches, 1,455 were scored by all seven judges.
Of 839 programs, 761 were scored by all six. Intervals resample issues or problems.

Unlike the held-out gap, neither new gap depends on the re-ask (exploratory). On LiveCodeBench, the outside judges also
rank Gemma's programs much worse than other writers' programs (a drop of 0.174 in AUROC), so every judge shares most of
Gemma's own drop there.

\paragraph{Limits of the LiveCodeBench data.} A Claude model read and ran 20 of Gemma's failing programs
(\path{data/livecodebench-pilot/hand-check/}). It found 14 real bugs. Three were grading errors: two float outputs
within the stated tolerance and one valid answer among several. Three were correct algorithms that run too slowly in
Python.

All LiveCodeBench problems predate 2025-04-07. DeepSeek-V3's pass rate falls from 77\% on problems from the
first half of 2023 to 16\% on the first half of 2025. Gemma's stays at 86 to 99\% in every half-year (answers cut off
at 2,000 tokens left out), so we cannot rule out that Gemma saw the problems in training. The
two other writers pass far fewer of the judged problems than Gemma (18\% and 24\%, against 50\%). Gemma's failing
programs are mostly on hard problems, and other writers' failing programs include problems Gemma solved. On the 146
problems Gemma failed, its leniency is still $+20.3$ [13.5, 27.1], and it gives 100 to 50\% of its own failing
programs against 12\% of the other writers' (exploratory, \path{scripts/lcb_matched_check.py}).

\paragraph{Placebo check (exploratory).} Some outside judges also give Gemma's failing items 100 more often than other
writers'. On SWE-bench Lite, Llama-4-Maverick gives 100 to 65\% of Gemma's failing patches and 43\% of other writers'.
We tested whether such judges are also lenient on Gemma's items. We treated each outside judge as if it had written
them and computed its leniency the same way, against the other judges except Gemma. Maverick's value is $-5.4$ % prose-ok: number-dense-paragraph
[$-10.1$, $-0.7$]. It gives high scores to many patches, but not more to Gemma's than the other judges do. On SWE-bench
Lite and LiveCodeBench, Gemma's leniency exceeds the largest of the outside judges' by 12.8 [4.3, 16.8] and 14.2 [3.3,
22.2] points. On the held-out issues, Kimi-K2 as judge is also lenient on Gemma's failing patches, at $+10.0$ [5.0,
14.9] against Gemma's $+16.6$. There Gemma exceeds the largest outside judge by 6.7 [$-2.7$, 12.2] points.

\subsection*{Five large models judging their own patches}

On the Mini issues, five of the other writers judged their own mini-SWE-agent patches, so the harness is matched for
them by construction. None shows a gap (Table~\ref{tab:five}). The pooled gap over the five is $-0.036$ [$-0.089$,
0.016].

We also measured their leniency on their own failing patches (Table~\ref{tab:fivelen}). The intervals come from the % prose-ok: number-dense-paragraph
same issue resamples as the gap. We computed them on 2026-10-22, after the point estimates had been seen, under a
plan that counts a model as lenient only if its interval stays above zero after a Bonferroni correction for five
models. Each model wrote only 14 to 24 failing patches, so the intervals are wide. The pooled leniency difference
over the five is $+5.1$ [$-0.8$, 11.0] points. Gemma's is $+20.9$ [11.5, 30.2] on the same issues. That is 15.8 [4.0, 27.7]
points above the pooled five. By the planned rule, none of the five is shown to be lenient.

Llama-4-Maverick comes closest. Its leniency difference is $+17.5$ [0.6, 32.9], and its Bonferroni interval [$-3.5$, % prose-ok: number-dense-sentence
38.4] includes zero. Maverick gives high scores to most failing patches, whoever wrote them. It scores 95 or above on
69\% of other writers' failing patches and on 57\% of its own. So it does not single out its own patches the way Gemma
does.

On the held-out issues, all nine judges also scored the five writers' own patches, which all come from mini-SWE-agent % prose-ok: number-dense-paragraph
(Table~\ref{tab:fiveheld}). Each writer has 46 to 123 failing patches there, three to six times as many as on the Mini
issues. We chose this analysis on 2026-10-22, after the Mini results above and a review of the paper
(\texttt{spec/other-models-heldout.md}). The code is the Mini code unchanged, and it reproduces Gemma's held-out gap
and leniency. The pooled gap over the five is $-0.001$ [$-0.031$, 0.029], and the
pooled leniency difference is $+0.4$ [$-2.5$, 3.4] points. Gemma exceeds the pooled five by 0.075 [0.015, 0.133] in gap
and by 16.3 [8.9, 23.4] points in leniency. By the same Bonferroni rule, none of the five is shown to have a gap or
leniency. Llama-4-Maverick's leniency difference falls to $+2.8$ [$-5.0$, 10.8], and it gives 100 to fewer of its own
failing patches than of other writers' (33\% against 42\%).

\section{Where the extra trust goes}
\label{app:edit}

This section backs Section 3.2 of the paper. Gemma could trust its patches because it recognizes them as its own, or
because it believes in the edit they make. The tests below point to the edit. None of them varies the writer with the
patch held fixed, so none rules out recognition.

\paragraph{Gemma is not lenient in general.} On other writers' failing held-out patches, Gemma's mean leniency is
$-2.6$ points, so it scores them slightly below the outside judges do (\path{derived/gemma_sizes_2026-10-28.json}).
Its extra trust is in its own failing patches only.

\paragraph{Patches the outside judges score the same (exploratory).} Gemma's failing patches do look a little better
to everyone. The outside judges give them a mean score of 57.2 on the held-out issues, against 46.8 for other writers'
failing patches. We therefore compared Gemma's own failing patches only with other writers' failing patches that the
outside judges score the same. We cut all failing patches into five bins of equal size by the outside judges' mean
score and weighted each bin by the number of Gemma's own failing patches in it
(\path{scripts/strong_writer_leniency.py}, \path{derived/strong_writer_leniency_2026-11-04.txt}). Gemma's leniency
difference is then $+13.6$ [7.1, 20.3] on the held-out issues and $+17.2$ [11.3, 23.0] on SWE-bench Lite, against
$+16.6$ and $+17.8$ without the match.

\subsection*{Other writers' failing patches that make Gemma's edit}

\paragraph{Test.} We fixed this test in a written plan before computing it (\path{spec/belief-vs-recognition.md},
exploratory). For each issue, we compared other writers' failing patches with Gemma's failing patch on the same issue.
A patch's edit is the set of lines it removes or adds in files that already exist, with whitespace stripped. A
patch makes Gemma's edit when the Jaccard similarity of its changed lines with Gemma's is at least 0.5. Two measures
use Gemma's excess score on each failing patch, its score minus the outside judges' mean. The first is the extra trust
in other writers' patches that make Gemma's edit over those that do not. The second is the extra trust in Gemma's own
failing patch over other writers' patches that make its edit, on issues that have both.

\paragraph{Reading fixed in the plan.} The edit explanation is supported on a data set if the first measure has an
interval above zero. The second measure must also be small: its interval includes zero, or its estimate is at most
half the paper's leniency on the same issues. Recognition is supported if the second measure's interval is above zero and its estimate
is more than half that leniency. With fewer than 20 same-edit patches, or any other pattern, the result is
inconclusive. The plan allowed the paper to state the edit explanation only if both data sets support it.

\paragraph{Result.} On SWE-bench Lite, Gemma gives other writers' failing patches that make its edit 20.0 points more
extra trust than the others, and the edit explanation is supported (Table~\ref{tab:edit}). On the held-out issues the
difference is 8.0 points, its interval reaches zero, and the result is inconclusive. On neither set do we detect extra
trust in Gemma's own patch beyond the same-edit patches. These are small comparisons: 16 held-out issues and 25 Lite
issues have both kinds of patch, and on the same 16 held-out issues Gemma's leniency on its own failing patches is
itself only $+0.6$. By the plan's rule, the edit explanation is supported on one data set of two. With a looser cut of
0.3, the first measure is $+6.2$ [$-0.7$, 12.5] on the held-out issues and $+17.4$ [11.8, 23.1] on Lite, and the
second is $+1.2$ and $-1.1$, both intervals including zero.

\subsection*{What Gemma writes when it rubber-stamps}

\paragraph{Gemma's reasons (exploratory).} Gemma scored 52 of its own failing held-out patches 100. At least two
outside judges scored 31 of these at 50 or less. We set this rule after a first look at the answers. For each
case, a coder found the main defect, the reason the low-scoring outside judges give for their low score, and asked
whether Gemma's answer names it (\texttt{data/hand-codes/}, with the codebook). Two coders coded every case on their own.
Both were Claude models: Sonnet, then Opus without access to the first coder's codes. The first coded 5 cases as
naming the defect and the second 6. They agree on 26 of 31 (Cohen's $\kappa = 0.45$): both find the defect never
mentioned in 23 and named in 3. Two of the five split cases come from choosing the main defect when the outside
judges name several. The other three turn on whether Gemma describing the flagged change, with no doubt about it,
counts as naming the defect.

The codebook also asked whether Gemma states the opposite of the outside judges' claim. The two coders
agree on this in only 13 of 31 cases ($\kappa = 0.03$), so we do not report it. One split case is django-15037. There Gemma
calls \texttt{to\_fields=[target\_field]} ``the correct way to specify a non-PK reference'', and GLM-4.5 (score 15)
answers that Django's keyword is \texttt{to\_field}. Two other low-scoring judges give a leftover script in the patch
as their reason, and Gemma mentions that script.

\paragraph{Gemma reads the defective line and calls it correct (exploratory).} Two more coders, Claude Sonnet models

\subsection*{Gemma's own reproduction scripts}

While fixing a held-out issue, Gemma often writes a script that reproduces the bug and runs it again after its edit.
Among the runs behind Gemma's 85 failing held-out patches, 64 ended on such a script: leaving out scripts that edit the
source, the last script Gemma ran is one its patch adds. In 58 of those 64, that last run ended without an error. So in
most failing runs Gemma had tested its patch with its own script, and the script passed. We counted the runs from the
logs of Gemma's agent runs (\path{scripts/paper_counts.py}, with the rule of \path{scripts/own_script_gate.py}).

\subsection*{A shell with the patch applied}

\paragraph{Setup.} We fixed the design and a stop rule in a written plan before any run (\path{spec/judge-as-actor.md},
exploratory). The judge was Gemma 4 31B, the 4-bit competition checkpoint served by vLLM on one NVIDIA
A100 at temperature 0 with thinking off. It judged each of the 133 held-out patches it had scored 100 as a reader
(52 failing, 81 passing). For each
patch we built a sandbox from the issue's SWE-bench image, with the repository's environment active, and applied the
patch without committing it. Gemma then ran in mini-SWE-agent 1.7.0 with a 15-step limit and was not told who wrote
the patch. Its task was the issue, the patch, and this instruction (\path{scripts/judge_actor.yaml}):

\begin{promptbox}\ttfamily
This patch has been applied to the repository in /testbed. Decide whether it resolves the issue. First write a test
that fails on the original code and passes with the patch applied; git stash and git stash pop switch between the two.
Then try at least one case the issue implies but does not show. Use the shell; do not decide from reading alone. When
done, end your answer with two lines: TEST: <one shell command that runs your test> and SCORE: <0 to 100, the chance
the patch passes the maintainers' hidden tests>.
\end{promptbox}

After the run, the harness, not Gemma, ran Gemma's \texttt{TEST} command twice in the same sandbox: once with the
patch reverted and once with it applied. A test run passes when it exits with code 0 and prints no traceback and no
assertion error. A first pilot ran without the repository's environment active, so Gemma could not import the code.
We discarded it and ran the pilot again with the environment fixed.

\paragraph{Stop rule.} The full run, with other writers' patches, would start only if the shell lowered Gemma's
approval enough. The plan stopped it if Gemma still gave 100 to more than half of the failing patches and to more
than 80\% of the passing ones.

\paragraph{Result.} Gemma gave a score in 132 of 133 runs. It still gives 100 to 41 of the 51 failing patches (80\%
[69, 90]) and to 77 of the 81 passing patches (95\% [90, 99]). The stop rule fired, so the full run did not start. In
31 of the 51 runs on failing patches (61\%), Gemma's test fails without the patch and passes with it. Its test checks
the behavior Gemma believes is right. The shell lowers Gemma's score on about one in five of these failing patches,
but also on one in twenty of the passing ones. So it separates them only slightly, with an AUROC of 0.58 [0.52, 0.64]
on the 132 patches. A run took 15 model calls on average and 140 seconds at the median
(\path{derived/judge_as_actor_2026-11-04.json}).

\subsection*{LoRA fine-tuning on labeled patches}

\paragraph{Setup.} We fixed the design and its reading in a written plan before training (\path{spec/sft-pilot.md},
with addendum 1, exploratory). The data are all labeled judging prompts of the held-out issues and SWE-bench Lite:
2,488 patches on 463 issues, by Gemma 4 31B and nine other writers. We split them by issue, never by patch: 40\% of
the issues for training, 20\% for development and 40\% for evaluation. The training set has 990 patches on 184
issues (450 passing, 540 failing, 190 of them Gemma's own). The evaluation set has 979 patches on 185 issues, with
528 failing.

\paragraph{Training.} Gemma's score is the logit of the word PASS minus the logit of FAIL after the paper's prompt. We trained
a rank-16 LoRA adapter on the deployed checkpoint, expanded to bf16, with binary cross-entropy on that margin, two
epochs, learning rate $2 \times 10^{-5}$ and one seed. Training took 3.1 A100-hours. Each condition, adapter on and
off, accepts a patch when its score clears a threshold set on the development issues to keep 90\% of their passing
patches.

\paragraph{Reading fixed in the plan.} Fine-tuning improves checking if three things hold on the evaluation issues.
False acceptance falls by at least 10 percentage points with an interval below zero, the adapter keeps at least 85\%
of the passing patches, and same-issue pair accuracy rises with an interval above zero. If the intervals of both false acceptance and pair
accuracy include zero, the reading is ``no measurable improvement''. Anything else is inconclusive.

\paragraph{Result.} The adapter does not reduce false acceptance by a measurable amount (Table~\ref{tab:lora}). It
accepts 48.9\% of evaluation failing patches against 53.0\% without it ($-4.2$ percentage points [$-8.3$, 0.0]), and it keeps
fewer passing patches. AUROC and same-issue pair accuracy do not change. Among Gemma's own failing evaluation patches
that it had scored 100 as a reader, from both data sets, the adapter still accepts 48 of 51, against 50 without it.
The reading is ``no measurable improvement''. One seed, one learning rate and 184 training issues do not show that
training cannot work.

\section{Tests that Gemma did not write}
\label{app:repair}

This section backs Section 3.4 of the paper. The repository's existing tests encode what the project's developers
expected. An agent can run them before and after a patch without a hidden test or a second model, hold back a patch
that breaks one, and show Gemma what broke.

\subsection*{The existing-test check}

\paragraph{Rule.} We fixed the check and its reading in a written plan before running it
(\path{spec/existing-test-gate.md}, \path{scripts/existing_test_gate.py}, exploratory). The check reads the patch and
the repository only. It never reads the hidden tests, the reference patch or any label.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Changed code.} The Python source files the patch modifies, and in them the innermost functions and
classes around each changed line.
\item \textbf{Tests.} The repository's test files, ranked by whether their name matches a changed module and by how
many changed functions or classes they name. The check keeps up to four, and a patch with no matching test file
has no check.
\item \textbf{Runs.} In the issue's SWE-bench sandbox, with the repository's test command, each selected file runs
twice on the unpatched code. A test that passes both times is stable. The selected files then run with the patch
applied, and again if a stable test fails.
\item \textbf{Decision.} The check holds back the patch if a stable test fails, or disappears, in both runs with the
patch. A patch with no stable test, or whose runs time out, is not held back.
\end{enumerate}
We ran it on all 957 held-out patches and on the reference patch of each of the 193 issues. It checked 161 of Gemma's
180 patches and 184 of the 193 reference patches. Over all 1,150 patches, 59 had no matching test, 26 had a run that
timed out or did not run, 8 had no stable test and 2 did not apply.

\paragraph{Reading fixed in the plan.} The check would count as a fix for an agent under three conditions. It holds back at
least 11 of the 52 failing patches Gemma scored 100, at most 4 of the 81 passing ones, and at most 5\% of the reference
patches.

\paragraph{Result.} The check holds back 15 of the 52 failing patches Gemma scored 100, and 26 of all 85 of Gemma's
failing patches (Table~\ref{tab:gate}). It also holds back 7 of the 81 passing patches Gemma scored 100 and 18 of the
193 reference patches. By the plan, the check alone holds back too many correct patches to count as a fix. Section 3.4
of the paper therefore uses it only to choose which patches get a repair run.

\paragraph{Real regressions and old tests.} After every decision was made, we ran each held-back patch's broken tests
once more with the reference patch applied. A broken test that passes with the reference patch is a real regression.
A broken test that also fails with the reference patch asserts the old behavior that the issue asks to change. For 14
of the 15 failing patches Gemma scored 100, the broken test passes with the reference patch, so these are real
regressions that Gemma missed. For 5 of the 7 passing patches Gemma scored 100, the broken tests also fail with the
reference patch. The 18 held-back reference patches are of this second kind by definition.

\subsection*{The repair run}

\paragraph{Setup.} We fixed the design and its reading in a written plan while the check was running, before any of
its decisions were read (\path{spec/test-repair.md}, exploratory). Every one of Gemma's 180 patches that the check held
back got one more Gemma run: 26 failing and 9 passing patches. Gemma 4 31B was served as for the shell judge (Section~\ref{app:checks}).
It ran in mini-SWE-agent 1.7.0 with its SWE-bench configuration, a 50-step limit, a 65,536-token context and the
repository's environment active. It started from the
issue's sandbox with the patch applied. Each patch ran in two conditions, with the same budget. The task was the issue
text followed by one note:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Broken tests shown.} ``A patch for this issue has already been applied to the repository (see
\texttt{git diff}). Running the repository's existing tests shows that the patch breaks tests that passed before it:''
Then, for each test file with a broken test, the command that runs it, the broken test names and the last 80 lines
of its output. Then ``Fix the patch so these tests pass again and the issue above stays solved. Do not edit the test
files.''
\item \textbf{Second look.} ``A patch for this issue has already been applied to the repository (see \texttt{git
diff}). It may contain mistakes or break existing behaviour. Check it and fix any problem you find, keeping the issue
above solved. Do not edit the test files.''
\end{itemize}
We graded the submitted patch with the hidden tests. A run that did not submit keeps the original patch and its
grade: 2 of 35 runs with the broken tests shown (model-call timeouts) and 1 of 35 second looks (the step limit).

\paragraph{Reading fixed in the plan.} The broken tests help repair if more held-back failing patches pass with the
broken tests shown than after a second look. The test is a one-sided exact sign test on the patches where the two
conditions differ, with $p < 0.05$.

\paragraph{Result.} With the broken tests shown, Gemma repairs 14 of the 26 held-back failing patches, against 6 after
a second look (Table~\ref{tab:repair}). Eight were repaired only with the tests shown and none only by the second look
($p = 0.004$), which meets the reading fixed before the run. Nine of the 14 are among the 52 failing patches Gemma
scored 100. The same step breaks 4 of the 9 held-back passing patches, all of them patches Gemma scored 100. The
second look breaks one, which Gemma had not scored 100. The sign test covers only the repair of held-back failing
patches. Over all 35 held-back patches, the run with the tests shown does better than the second look on 9
and worse on 4 ($p = 0.13$, exploratory). Among the 133 patches Gemma scored 100, it does better on 6 and worse on 4
($p = 0.38$).

\paragraph{Why repairs fail and why correct patches break.} We read every repair run that ended failing and every
broken correct patch (\path{notes/repair-trace-audit-2026-11-05.md}, \path{scripts/repair_trace_audit_2026_11_05.py}).
Of the 12 held-back failing patches that stay failing with the tests shown, 11 were submitted by Gemma after 7 to 38
model calls, with steps left, and one ended in a model-call timeout. So the runs did not fail for lack of steps.

The most common failure is a conflict between the issue and an old test. The note asks Gemma to make the broken tests pass and
keep the issue solved, and when an old test asserts the behavior the issue changes, Gemma often restores the old
behavior. In 3 of the 4 correct patches it broke, the broken test also fails with the reference patch, and Gemma
restored the old behavior to make it pass. The fourth, django-11276, was broken by an editing error. Other failed
repairs come from editing errors and from edits Gemma did not check. On sympy issue
12481 Gemma wrote that it had ``re-introduced the disjointness check to make \texttt{test\_args} pass, although this
contradicts the PR description''.

\subsection*{Breaking the tie at 100 by fewer model calls}

Section 3.4 of the paper closes with a way to set Gemma's verdict aside without repairing anything. We fixed three
tie-breaks on the Mini issues before computing any held-out value (Table~\ref{tab:tiebreak}; \texttt{spec/tie-break.md}).
Inside Gemma's score, each ranks one kind of patch higher: fewer concern words in Gemma's answer, a shorter answer, or
fewer model calls in Gemma's agent run. Only our own runs record model calls, so that tie-break was chosen on the 32
matched-harness Mini patches Gemma scored 100. On the held-out issues, concern words do not help, a shorter answer
helps a little, and fewer model calls help most.

Ranking Gemma's 180 held-out patches by its score and then by fewer
model calls raises the share of the top quarter that passes from 61\% to 78\%. The rule ``Gemma scores 100 and the
run used at most 9 model calls'' submits 41 held-out patches (22.8\%). Of these, 78.0\% pass. Inside the tie, the
number of model calls correlates with the outside judges' mean score (Spearman $-0.57$), so run length may partly
stand for how hard the issue is.
