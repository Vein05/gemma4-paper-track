\section{Setup details and judge prompt}
\label{app:prompt}

\paragraph{Issues.} The held-out issues are the first 200 of a fixed random order (seed 0) of SWE-bench Verified issues
outside the Mini issues for which at least one of the five other writers has a usable patch (447 issues). Five pilot
issues drawn first were left out of the sample. A usable patch is non-empty after removing test files and at most
30,000 characters long.

\paragraph{Agent.} The 4-bit competition checkpoint \texttt{gemma-4-31B-it-qat-w4a16-ct} (revision \texttt{52f3f65b}) ran
in mini-SWE-agent v1.7.0 with its SWE-bench configuration unchanged (250 steps, temperature 0, 60-second command
timeout), served by vLLM with a 131,072-token context on one NVIDIA L40S. Docker was replaced by Apptainer sandboxes
of the same SWE-bench images; grading used SWE-bench 4.1.0's grading code in the same kind of sandbox.

\paragraph{Judges.} Outside judges for a model exclude its own family and, for each patch, the family of the patch's % prose-ok: number-dense-paragraph
writer. Gemma as judge is the hosted model. OpenRouter routed its 1,064 held-out calls to nine providers. Most of them
(983) went to providers that listed 4-bit or 8-bit weights on 2026-10-05. Of the rest, 79 went to providers that listed
no precision and 2 to a provider that served bf16. We did not record the precision of each call (provider per call: \path{scripts/derived/fresh_gemma4-31b.jsonl}).
In an earlier one-word check on 173 other patches, the hosted model pinned to a bf16 provider and the 4-bit checkpoint
gave the same answer in 331 of 344 calls. The re-ask limit was 8,000 output tokens for our own
patch sets and 3,000 for the mixed-harness set; other writers' patches on the Mini issues keep their mixed-harness
scores.

\paragraph{Gemma as judge, served locally.} We repeated the held-out judging with the competition checkpoint itself,
served by vLLM 0.30.0 on one NVIDIA A100 at temperature 0 with thinking off, under a plan fixed before any score
(\path{spec/gemma-31b-local-judge.md}). It gave a score to all 1,063 prompts on the first ask (rows:
\path{scripts/derived/fresh_gemma4-31b-local.jsonl}).

On the paper's patches, the local judge gives 100 to 54\% of Gemma's failing patches and to 32\% of other writers'. % prose-ok: number-dense-paragraph
The hosted model gives 61\% and 35\%. On failing patches, the local judge's leniency difference is $+13.4$
[6.9, 20.0]. For the hosted model it is $+16.6$ [10.3, 23.1]. The paired difference, hosted minus local, is $+3.2$
[$-0.5$, 7.2]. We detect no difference between the two judges, but the interval allows the hosted model to be up to
7.2 points more lenient. On passing patches, the local judge's leniency difference is $+5.0$ [0.8, 9.0]. For the
hosted model it is $+3.4$ [$-2.0$, 8.0]. The local judge's AUROC is 0.658 on Gemma's patches and 0.762 on other
writers'. For the hosted model they are 0.622 and 0.751.

\paragraph{Prompt.} Every judge received the same system and user messages at temperature 0
(\path{scripts/selfjudge_matrix.py}, \path{scripts/selection_test.py}). Braces mark the parts filled in per patch.
\begin{promptbox}\ttfamily
\textbf{System:} You are an expert Python maintainer. You review a proposed fix for a reported issue. You cannot run
code; judge from the material given.\par\medskip
\textbf{User:} A user reported the issue below for the repository \{owner/repo\}.\par
<issue>\{issue text\}</issue>\par
A coding agent produced the patch below.\par
<patch>\{patch without test files\}</patch>\par
Question: after this patch is applied, will the maintainers' hidden tests for this issue pass? The hidden tests were
written for the upstream fix. They check the behaviour the issue asks for, and tests that passed before must still
pass. Explain briefly, then end your reply with a final line that is exactly SCORE: <n>, where n is your probability
from 0 to 100 that the hidden tests pass.
\end{promptbox}


\section{Agent runs and grading}
\label{app:grading}

\paragraph{Agent runs.} The 200 held-out issues ran as two jobs of 100. In the first, 98 runs submitted a patch, one
ran out of the 131,072-token context and one stopped after repeated model-call timeouts; three patches were empty. In the second, one submitted patch was empty. On the Mini issues, 49 runs
submitted and one stopped after repeated timeouts. Empty patches and runs that stopped count as unresolved, as in
SWE-bench. One Mini issue (\texttt{django\_\_django-11848}) failed to start its sandbox and was run again; the Mini
analyses use the second run's patch.

\paragraph{Reference-patch check.} We graded the official reference patch of every issue we ran (5 pilot, 200
held-out, 50 Mini). 248 of 255 resolve in our environment. The first pass labelled 16 matplotlib and xarray issues as
timeouts; logs showed the container build, not the tests, exceeded a 1,200-second limit, and with a 3,600-second build
limit all but three resolved. The 7 issues whose reference patch does not resolve were dropped from the held-out set
(\texttt{astropy-7606}, \texttt{matplotlib-20826}, \texttt{-23314}, \texttt{-26113}, \texttt{-26208},
\texttt{requests-2931}, \texttt{sphinx-9711}); Gemma resolved none of them.

\paragraph{Agreement with official labels.} Before using our grading, we graded 30 gpt-oss-120b patches with known
official labels, stratified by repository. 26 of 30 matched. The four misses were environment failures (a
network-dependent test, Ghostscript file access) and patches that apply only with fuzzy matching on today's rebuilt
images. Other writers' patches therefore keep their official labels.

\paragraph{Resolve rates.} Gemma resolved 56 and 42 of the two held-out batches of 100, 98 of the 193 kept issues in
all, and 22 of the 50 Mini issues. On the Mini issues two sphinx patches ran past the 1,800-second test limit;
counting them as failing, as SWE-bench does, changes the matched-harness gap from $+0.066$ to $+0.081$ [$-0.010$,
0.178].

\section{What the deficit is not}
\label{app:mechanism}


We checked four simple explanations on the mixed-harness patches, where Gemma has several attempts per issue.

\paragraph{Patch size.} Gemma's patches are much smaller than the other writers' (7.7 against 72.7 added lines on
average). Among single-file failing patches, matched by size in four bins, Gemma's leniency on its own patches stays
24.1 points [12.7, 39.9] above its leniency on other writers' patches (Table~\ref{tab:size}).

\paragraph{Missing reasoning.} We rated every Mini patch again with Gemma's thinking switched on
(Table~\ref{tab:think}). Its AUROC on its own patches does not move. It also gives 100 to the same share of its own
failing patches: 48 of 79 with thinking off and 49 with it on, 40 of them in both. Thinking makes its other scores
more extreme. The own failing patches it does not score 100 drop from a mean of 34 to 14 ($-19.7$ [$-34.0$, $-4.5$]).
Its mean leniency on its own failing patches therefore falls from $+12.9$ to $+6.2$ points. On other writers' failing
patches it rises from $-8.1$ to $-5.5$. The self-judging gap shrinks because thinking makes Gemma less accurate on
other writers' patches.

Chen et al.\ (2025, cited in the paper) count how often a judge prefers its own wrong answer to another model's
correct one. Our closest measure pairs Gemma's failing patch with another writer's passing patch on the same issue.
There are only 52 such pairs. Gemma scores its own patch higher in 17\% of them with thinking off and in 12\% with it
on, and the two tie in 42\% and 46\%.

\paragraph{Issues it cannot solve.} Gemma scores 66\% of its failing patches at 95 or above on issues that one of its
attempts solved (29 patches) and 67\% on issues none solved (51). On other writers' failing patches for the unsolved
issues, it is slightly stricter than outside judges ($-5.8$ points). So ``a model cannot verify what it cannot solve''
does not fit.

\paragraph{What Gemma writes when it rubber-stamps (exploratory).} Gemma scored 52 of its own failing held-out patches
100. At least two outside judges scored 31 of these at 50 or less. We set this rule after a first look at the answers. For each
case, a coder found the main defect, the reason the low-scoring outside judges give for their low score, and asked
whether Gemma's answer names it (\texttt{data/hand-codes/}, with the codebook). Two coders coded every case on their own.
Both were Claude models: Sonnet, then Opus without access to the first coder's codes. The first coded 5 cases as
naming the defect and the second 6. They agree on 26 of 31 (Cohen's $\kappa = 0.45$): both find the defect never
mentioned in 23 and named in 3. Two of the five split cases come from choosing the main defect when the outside
judges name several. The other three turn on whether Gemma describing the flagged change, with no doubt about it,
counts as naming the defect.

The codebook also asked whether Gemma states the opposite of the outside judges' claim. The two coders
agree on this in only 13 of 31 cases ($\kappa = 0.03$), so we do not report it. One split case is django-15037. There Gemma
calls \texttt{to\_fields=[target\_field]} ``the correct way to specify a non-PK reference'', and GLM-4.5 (score 15)
answers that Django's keyword is \texttt{to\_field}. Two other low-scoring judges give a leftover script in the patch
as their reason, and Gemma mentions that script.
\texttt{derived/rubber\_stamp\_reasons\_2026-10-22.json}.

\paragraph{Choosing among attempts.} We split the pass/fail pairs of Gemma's own patches into pairs from the same issue
and pairs from different issues and computed pair accuracy, with ties counted as half. On 47 same-issue pairs Gemma
scores 0.628 against 0.646 for the outside judges ($-0.019$ [$-0.243$, 0.148]). On 5,553 different-issue pairs it
scores 0.636 against 0.706 ($-0.070$ [$-0.138$, $-0.006$]). Gemma gives exactly 100 to 110 of its 150 own patches, so
its scores carry little information about which issue a patch belongs to; breaking those ties perfectly would raise
its AUROC from 0.64 to 0.91.

We cannot yet tell whether Gemma recognizes its own patches or shares the mistakes in them. It is not more lenient
toward other writers' failing patches that resemble its own wrong fix on the same issue ($+5.6$ [$-5.5$, 17.4]), but
most of those patches share almost no changed lines with Gemma's, so this test is weak.

\section{Models judging their own patches}
\label{app:five}


On the Mini issues, five of the other writers judged their own mini-SWE-agent patches, so the harness is matched for
them by construction. None shows a gap (Table~\ref{tab:five}). The pooled gap over the five is $-0.036$ [$-0.089$,
0.016].

We also measured their leniency on their own failing patches (Table~\ref{tab:fivelen}). The intervals come from the % prose-ok: number-dense-paragraph
same issue resamples as the gap. We computed them on 2026-10-22, after the point estimates had been seen, under a
plan that counts a model as lenient only if its interval stays above zero after a Bonferroni correction for five
models. Each model wrote only 14 to 24 failing patches, so the intervals are wide. The pooled leniency difference
over the five is $+5.1$ [$-0.8$, 11.0] points. Gemma's is $+20.9$ [11.5, 30.2] on the same issues. That is 15.8 [4.0, 27.7]
points above the pooled five. By the planned rule, none of the five is shown to be lenient.

Llama-4-Maverick comes closest. Its leniency difference is $+17.5$ [0.6, 32.9], and its Bonferroni interval [$-3.5$, % prose-ok: number-dense-sentence
38.4] includes zero. Maverick gives high scores to most failing patches, whoever wrote them. It scores 95 or above on
69\% of other writers' failing patches and on 57\% of its own. So it does not single out its own patches the way Gemma
does.

On the held-out issues, all nine judges also scored the five writers' own patches, which all come from mini-SWE-agent % prose-ok: number-dense-paragraph
(Table~\ref{tab:fiveheld}). Each writer has 46 to 123 failing patches there, three to six times as many as on the Mini
issues. We chose this analysis on 2026-10-22, after the Mini results above and a review of the paper
(\texttt{spec/other-models-heldout.md}). The code is the Mini code unchanged, and it reproduces Gemma's held-out gap
and leniency in the paper (Sections 3.1 and 3.3). The pooled gap over the five is $-0.001$ [$-0.031$, 0.029], and the
pooled leniency difference is $+0.4$ [$-2.5$, 3.4] points. Gemma exceeds the pooled five by 0.075 [0.015, 0.133] in gap
and by 16.3 [8.9, 23.4] points in leniency. By the same Bonferroni rule, none of the five is shown to have a gap or
leniency. Llama-4-Maverick's leniency difference falls to $+2.8$ [$-5.0$, 10.8], and it gives 100 to fewer of its own
failing patches than of other writers' (33\% against 42\%).

\section{Gemma's own signals}
\label{app:selffix}

\paragraph{Breaking the tie at 100.} We fixed three tie-breaks on the Mini issues before computing any held-out
value (Table~\ref{tab:tiebreak}; \texttt{spec/tie-break.md}). Inside Gemma's score, each ranks one kind of patch
higher: fewer concern words in Gemma's answer, a shorter answer, or fewer model calls in Gemma's agent run. Only our
own runs record model calls, so that tie-break was chosen on the 32 matched-harness Mini patches Gemma scored 100. On
the held-out issues, concern words do not help, a shorter answer helps a little, and fewer model calls help most. The
rule ``Gemma scores 100 and the run used at most 9 model calls'' submits 41 held-out patches (22.8\%). Of these,
78.0\% pass. Inside the tie, the number of model calls correlates with the outside judges' mean score (Spearman
$-0.57$), so run length may partly stand for how hard the issue is. Against this tie-break (exploratory), GLM-4.5's
top quarter passes $+5.9$ points more [$-3.6$, 16.4] and its top half $+1.9$ [$-5.2$, 10.4]. Kimi-K2's differ by
$-0.8$ [$-10.7$, 10.7] and $+0.7$ [$-6.6$, 9.1].
